## Original proposal

**Field Atlas:** an interactive planetary atlas on a pale mineral surface. A centered real Earth remains the artifact; the rest feels like an exact scientific publication. This replaces the repeated dark-globe-over-two-sans-lines composition.

Masthead: small NASA mark/project identity left, Features and Try AirLLM right. Oversized Newsreader display reads **“A planet of changing patterns.”** around Earth, with only decorative middle letters occluded. A front-layer sentence explains regional data and statistical testing. The complete semantic heading remains legible. Globe occupies 40–46% viewport height, with thin geographic grid, no fake coordinate streams or fabricated metrics.

Palette: mineral cream `#e9e7dd`, pine-black `#173a34`, warm white, and one vermilion action `#db583d`. Newsreader medium carries display; Geist Sans handles interface and body; monospace is reserved for actual schemas. The ocean supplies blue. The compact smoked-cream glass prompt has a circular vermilion send control and DigiTwin immediately beneath.

Twin entry rolls the same globe 180 degrees. Mineral cream becomes twilight ink, display becomes apricot, and only the text plane briefly defocuses. Heading: **“A possible Earth.”** Persistent status: **“Simulation under your assumptions.”** Return restores observed question, location and evidence.

Below: one compact question→record→evidence spread, followed by an investigation **case sheet** when requested: region/period title, wide plot, verdict margin, provenance and evidence index. Twin forms become a **scenario sheet**: choose/create, supply record, confirm assumptions, then clearly simulated reference/ranges. The unavailable-service state remains honest.

AirLLM becomes a **local field guide**: a precise question→backend→local model→checked-text diagram and concise installation steps with copy controls and official download; no fake terminal feed.

Acceptance: first viewport fits heading, full Earth, prompt and switch at 390px. Preserve one renderer, zero paused/offscreen draws, bounded DPR, lazy charts, keyboard controls and reduced motion. Risk: pale ground can make Earth look cut out; use real terminator lighting and a quiet grounding shadow, not a giant halo. Serif display is intentionally less conventional “tech”; the globe and exact interface carry the technical subject.

## Debate and final vote

**A's Orbital Folio:** the continuous Earth-to-evidence story is useful. Its charcoal stage and another dramatic type horizon, however, remain too close to the twice-rejected dark globe presentation. Spatial spectacle must not become onboarding work or hide a question behind an exhibit. I keep its continuity principle and reject its stage as the site's identity.

**C's Atlas Desk:** strongest at task clarity, units and actual working states. Cobalt all-caps type and an instrument-centered composition could nevertheless resemble another GIS application. The product is approachable science for students, not a professional mapping console. Its evidence-sheet discipline belongs inside Living Atlas, not its whole visual vocabulary.

**Vote: Living Atlas.** It offers a new cultural home for this exact product and carries every route, not just the hero. A is right that ivory and serif alone could look like a travel magazine. The implementation counters that with direct regional-data/testing copy, real controls, correct source context, visible scenario assumptions, and an interactive physical Earth. No parchment texture, fake annotations, book props or generic sustainability claims.
